Origins of TANK.EXE from the 90s

Back in those days, I was a student at the Faculty of Automation and Computer Engineering at the Moscow Power Engineering Institute (MPEI), studying to become a software engineer.

To get closer to real computers, I took a job at the Student Design Bureau (SKB). There, we developed software for power station generator control panels, educational and laboratory applications, and even training games for electricians. Our workhorses were then-state-of-the-art IBM PC/AT 386SX machines equipped with color VGA monitors and 3.5-inch floppy drives.

Floppy Disks & High-Stakes Tournaments

Naturally, we brought games on floppy disks and fired them up during lunch breaks and late into the evening — mostly popular side-scrollers, shooters, and adventure quests. But three titles generated unmatched competitive frenzy: Filler, Dynablaster, and simply TANK.EXE. You can still easily find articles and videos about the first two on the Internet, but the original TANK.EXE seems to have vanished completely into oblivion.

All three games supported two players on a single keyboard, and we hosted fierce tournaments. As university students, we received subsidized cafeteria meal coupons, purchased at a fraction of their actual cost (each ticket covered a full hot lunch). Since gambling for money was strictly forbidden — and cash was scarce anyway — meal coupons became our tournament currency!

Some players had greater luck and accumulated a massive stack of meal coupons. Since it was physically impossible for one person to eat through all that food alone, the lucky champion would treat the entire crew at a commercial bistro next to the cafeteria, where coupons could be traded for succulent roasted chicken or shish kebabs.

The "Gentlemen's Agreement" & Banned Tactics

We quickly uncovered every subtlety of the game, mastering its physics and timing down to pure motor muscle memory and developing dominant strategies. Battles grew so intense that certain maneuvers were eventually banned by an unspoken convention as unsportsmanlike, because there was no viable defense against them:

  • In Dynablaster, dropping bombs onto an opponent's spawn tile 3–4 seconds before their respawn was banned, as the player had no time to dodge out of the blast radius.
  • In TANK.EXE, planting landmines directly in front of the enemy base gate was strictly outlawed — otherwise the respawning tank was obliterated the instant it emerged.

The Mystery Creator & Revival in Antigravity

TANK.EXE was an exceptional game featuring remarkably crisp visuals for its time and sublime gameplay, crafted by a completely unknown developer. I analyzed its binary executable: compiler signatures pointed to Borland Pascal 4.x, yet it contained no credits, handles, or author names whatsoever.

Unable to find any trace of the game or its history online, I decided to recreate my own version using AI in Antigravity — as a heartfelt homage to its anonymous creator and to bring those cherished memories back to life.

And it succeeded! Playing it today evokes the exact same thrill and reawakens the exact same reflexes and motor skills from 30 years ago.

Beyond a Classic Remake

To make this something greater than just a nostalgic replica, the classic formula was expanded with modern enhancements:

  • Single Player vs. Computer AI — an intelligent bot opponent featuring three distinct difficulty levels and tactical pathfinding;
  • Battlefield Obstacles — destructible urban houses providing cover and impassable moats;
  • Save and Load Game slots, comprehensive post-match battle statistics, and fully rebindable controls.

Enjoy the game!
